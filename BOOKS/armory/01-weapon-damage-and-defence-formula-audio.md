Chapter One. Weapon Damage and the Defence Formula. Audio rendition of the approved chapter. Same content, same numbers, formatted as continuous prose for a text to speech reader rather than for the page.

Attack rolls are taught in full in the Player's Handbook, in the Attacks section. Melee weapons and lightsabers use Strength for the attack roll. Ranged weapons use Dexterity for the attack roll. This chapter does not re-teach that roll. It tells you what a weapon's own stat line adds once that roll is made.

Here is how damage works, by category. A one-handed melee weapon adds its wielder's Strength to its damage. A two-handed melee weapon adds one and a half times Strength, rounded down. A ranged weapon adds Dexterity to its damage, as established in the equipment rules. A lightsaber adds Strength to its damage as well, but this is provisional rather than settled. The source material calls lightsabers, quote, not melee weapons, end quote, and treating a lightsaber as melee for damage purposes is a working assumption rather than a confirmed rule. If that assumption turns out to be wrong, it is worth about three points of damage either way on every hit a Jedi lands, so it matters, but it has not been proven wrong, and until it is, this is how a lightsaber's damage is calculated.

Every weapon in this book belongs to one of six wield classes, and the class determines whether the weapon can be paired with another weapon in the off hand.

Class one is one handed light. The Stun Baton is the only weapon in this class, and it can be paired.

Class two is one handed. It includes the Long Sword, the Vibrosword, the Short Sword, the Vibroblade, the Lightsaber, and the Short Lightsaber. All of these can be paired.

Class three is two handed staff weapons. It includes the Quarterstaff, the Gaffi Stick, the Wookiee Warblade, the Double Bladed Sword, the Vibro Double Blade, and the Double Bladed Lightsaber. None of these can be paired with a second weapon, because each one already counts as two weapons in a single frame.

Class four is pistols. It includes the Blaster, the Heavy Blaster, the Hold Out Blaster, the Ion Blaster, the Disruptor Pistol, and the Sonic Pistol. All of these can be paired.

Class five is rifles. It includes the Ion Rifle, the Bowcaster, the Carbine, the Disruptor Rifle, the Sonic Rifle, and the Blaster Rifle. None of these can be paired.

Class six is heavy weapons. It includes the Repeating Blaster and the Heavy Repeating Blaster. Neither can be paired.

On critical hits: a weapon's threat range is the roll on the attack die that scores a critical hit. For example, a threat range of nineteen to twenty means that rolling either a nineteen or a twenty on the attack die scores a critical. A weapon's multiplier, such as times two or times three, applies to the weapon's damage dice when a critical hit lands. It does not apply to any ability score bonus added to that damage. Some unique weapons grant bonus damage on a critical hit on top of the multiplier. That bonus damage, called a Massive Critical, is capped at two six sided dice, regardless of how much higher the source game allowed it to go for that particular weapon.

Now, Defence. A character's Defence equals ten, plus their armour bonus, plus their Dexterity modifier, and that Dexterity contribution is capped depending on how heavy the armour is.

For organic armour, one simple rule generates the entire table: the armour bonus and the maximum Dexterity bonus always add up to nine. An armour class of four gives a bonus of four and allows a maximum Dexterity bonus of five. An armour class of five gives a bonus of five and allows a maximum Dexterity bonus of four. An armour class of six gives a bonus of six and allows a maximum Dexterity bonus of three. An armour class of seven gives a bonus of seven and allows a maximum Dexterity bonus of two. An armour class of eight gives a bonus of eight and allows a maximum Dexterity bonus of one. And an armour class of nine gives a bonus of nine and allows no Dexterity bonus at all. The first two of these, at armour class four and five, are Light armour, made of leather. The rest are Medium or Heavy armour.

There are two known exceptions among organic armour, both from the source game. The Armoured Flight Suit follows the rule exactly, with a bonus of five and a maximum Dexterity bonus of four. The Zeison Sha armour does not follow the rule. It gives a bonus of three and allows a maximum Dexterity bonus of four, which adds up to seven rather than nine.

Robes are the exception that actually matters in play. A robe's contribution to Defence from Dexterity has no cap at all. Consider a Jedi wearing a Master Robe, which gives a bonus of three, with a Dexterity score of twenty eight, which gives a Dexterity modifier of nine. That Jedi reaches a Defence of twelve, which is higher than any suit of armour in the game can produce, and the Jedi pays for it only by giving up whatever armour bonus they are not wearing instead. This is why Jedi wear robes.

Droid plating is a separate category of item, and it never followed the sum of nine rule that organic armour follows. In the first game, Light Plating gives a bonus of three and a maximum Dexterity bonus of six. Medium Plating gives a bonus of four and a maximum Dexterity bonus of three. Heavy Plating gives a bonus of nine and a maximum Dexterity bonus of one. In the second game, Light and Medium Plating both become uncapped on Dexterity entirely, while Heavy Plating stays the same, at a bonus of nine and a maximum Dexterity bonus of one. The reason Light and Medium read as uncapped in the second game rather than showing some other small number is that the game's own data uses a specific negative value as a signal meaning, quote, no cap at all, end quote, rather than meaning a negative bonus.

Two restrictions carry over from the source material and are not stated anywhere else. Organic armour cannot be worn by droids, who wear plating instead, and it cannot be worn by Wookiees. And wearing armour blocks the use of many Force powers, a restriction that is already load bearing elsewhere in this game's rules, specifically in how heavy armour and the Soresu lightsaber form interact with a class feature called Well Guarded.

Two open items are carried forward from this chapter's review. First, a chapter of the Player's Handbook currently states an outdated version of how ranged weapons add to damage, and that has been reported separately rather than fixed inside this chapter. Second, the rule that a lightsaber's damage uses Strength is marked provisional in its own source material, as already explained above, and that flag is being carried forward rather than smoothed over into a stated fact.
